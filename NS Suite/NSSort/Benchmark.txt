WHY WE LOSE: UNIFORM

Uniform random int64 spans the full 64-bit range. Every byte position
of every value is independently chaotic. There is no structure to exploit.

NSSort uses 8-pass LSD radix on this path: 8 bits per pass, 256 buckets,
fully parallel. For full int64 range, all 8 passes are mandatory. Each pass
moves 800MB of data between two buffers. Total memory traffic: 6.4GB minimum.

IPS4o is an in-place samplesort. No double-buffer. 3-4 recursive levels on
uniform. Less total memory traffic by design. On structureless data, memory
bandwidth is the only battlefield and in-place wins.

This is not a tuning gap. It is a structural one.


WHY WE LOSE: EXPONENTIAL

Exponential data has a dense cluster at the low end and a sparse tail spanning
the full int64 range. The tail forces full-range radix passes. The dense cluster
is handled efficiently but the tail dominates at 100M scale. A MSB-first pass
splitting dense vs sparse before radix on the tail would help: left as a future
optimization. Structural loss, not a routing failure.

ON IPS4o

IPS4o is an exceptional piece of engineering. Branchless classification,
cache-efficient block scatter, careful NUMA-aware parallelism, adaptive
bucket count. The authors benchmarked against 20+ competing implementations
across 4 machine types. It deserves its reputation.

Of all the competitors across the NS Suite, IPS4o taught us the most.
Every other benchmark opponent had a clear structural gap that the NS
methodology exposed quickly. IPS4o had gaps too: but it fought back at
every level. It forced us to understand our own algorithm more deeply than
we expected to. The two distributions where it wins are the two where it
genuinely should. That is not a flaw in NSSort. That is IPS4o doing its job.

We are grateful for the benchmark. It made NSSort better.

NSSort winning against IPS4o on 7 of 10 distributions by exploiting structure IPS4o
cannot assume. On the 3 distributions with no exploitable structure, IPS4o
wins. That is the correct outcome.

PASS-SKIP OPTIMIZATION

For inputs that do not span the full 64-bit range, NSSort skips radix passes
where all values share the same byte. A 32-bit range reduces to 4 passes.
This does not help uniform (all 8 bytes active) but benefits other wide-range
distributions.
